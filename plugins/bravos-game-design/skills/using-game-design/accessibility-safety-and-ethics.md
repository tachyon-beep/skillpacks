# Accessibility, Safety, and Ethics

Use this reference whenever play can exclude, pressure, expose, exhaust, injure,
monetize, manipulate, or otherwise materially affect participants. Integrate
these constraints into mechanics, facilitation, business model, and testing from
the start.

## Contents

- Map participation barriers
- Design access into the interaction
- Assess safety and consent
- Handle social power and conduct
- Avoid manipulative economies
- Protect privacy and research participants
- Treat representation responsibly

## Map participation barriers

For every load-bearing action, ask who must:

- see color, contrast, scale, motion, depth, symbols, faces, or distant state;
- hear speech, direction, tone, music, alerts, or simultaneous conversation;
- read, remember, calculate, plan, switch attention, infer conventions, or act
  under time pressure;
- hold, reach, drag, tap, aim, balance, move, stand, run, tolerate contact, or
  sustain repetitive input;
- speak, improvise, perform emotion, deceive, negotiate, interrupt, or withstand
  public evaluation;
- understand a language, cultural reference, genre convention, or specialized
  vocabulary;
- attend at a fixed time, remain for the duration, travel, own equipment, pay,
  create an account, or expose personal data.

Map the barrier to consequence. An optional flourish differs from the only path
to agency, safety, victory, social belonging, or comprehension.

Include setup, onboarding, breaks, re-entry, spectatorship, facilitation,
cleanup, and withdrawal. Access to the first turn is not access to the whole
experience.

## Design access into the interaction

Prefer multiple equivalent channels when the channel is not the intended skill:

- combine color with shape, label, position, or pattern;
- combine audio with caption, visual signal, haptic cue, or transcript;
- support remapping, hold/toggle choices, timing windows, pause, repetition,
  scaling, difficulty dimensions, and assistive devices;
- externalize memory and calculation that are not the tested skill;
- make critical state reachable from normal positions and devices;
- offer communication channels that do not require speed, volume, eye contact,
  or improvisational confidence;
- provide seated, low-contact, low-sensory, remote, or observer-to-participant
  routes when they preserve the experience.

Do not add a setting that technically permits access while making it socially
stigmatizing, competitively inferior, or hidden. Test with people who use the
mode, compensate their expertise, and avoid treating one person as representative
of a population.

When an ability is the intended skill—rhythm, spatial memory, bluffing,
improvisation, endurance—state that honestly. Explore variants that preserve the
core challenge without treating exclusion as proof of purity.

## Assess safety and consent

Map hazards across:

- physical injury, fatigue, contact, environment, equipment, and emergency
  access;
- sensory overload, disorientation, flashing, motion, sound, darkness, and
  confinement;
- emotional intensity, horror, humiliation, intimacy, grief, trauma, and bleed;
- social conflict, targeting, deception, exclusion, harassment, retaliation,
  and community reputation;
- financial loss, gambling-like reinforcement, scarcity pressure, and spending;
- privacy, surveillance, location, recordings, chat, identity, and persistent
  records.

For each material hazard, specify:

```text
hazard -> exposed participants -> likelihood -> severity -> prevention
       -> calibration -> stop/exit -> response -> follow-up
```

Eliminate or substitute hazards before relying on warnings. A disclaimer does
not make an avoidable risk necessary.

Treat consent as specific, informed, reversible, and affected by power. Consent
to a game is not blanket consent to every topic, touch, recording, purchase,
role, disclosure, or escalation. Make refusal and withdrawal legible,
accessible, and non-punitive in rules and social practice.

Never require a participant to remain in fiction or explain personal reasons to
invoke a safety boundary. Do not impose retaliatory, humiliating, financial, or
social penalties. When withdrawal necessarily changes a competitive result,
use a predeclared fair resolution such as replacement, reschedule, forfeit,
no-contest, or preserved result as appropriate; make that consequence clear
without turning it into pressure to remain.

## Design calibration as an operational mechanic

Choose signals and procedures that participants can actually perceive,
remember, perform, and trust. Provide ways to:

- increase or invite intensity;
- confirm the current level;
- reduce, redirect, or skip content;
- pause or step out;
- stop play immediately; and
- seek private or later support.

Define who responds and what they do. Test the procedure in representative
conditions: noise, darkness, mobility limits, remote channels, authority
differences, and time pressure.

Do not advertise a tool without supporting its use. If invocation creates
argument, disbelief, attention, disadvantage, or organizer annoyance, the tool
is not functioning.

## Account for social power and conduct

Separate fictional permission from participant permission. A villainous role,
competitive objective, improvisational prompt, or “anything goes” fiction does
not authorize harassment, discrimination, unwanted intimacy, real-world threats,
or exploitation of private knowledge.

Check:

- who can refuse whom without social or competitive cost;
- whether organizers, facilitators, streamers, community leaders, or experienced
  players receive de facto immunity;
- whether anonymous, private, or delayed reporting exists;
- who investigates and responds to conduct problems;
- whether moderation tools cover voice, text, images, user-generated content,
  proximity, and off-platform coordination;
- whether ranking, voting, expulsion, or social deduction exposes marginalized
  participants to patterned targeting;
- whether children or vulnerable adults require additional safeguards.

Do not ask safety tools to solve misconduct that requires policy, moderation,
staffing, or removal.

## Avoid manipulative economies

Inspect monetization and engagement systems for:

- variable-ratio rewards, paid randomization, near misses, and gambling-like
  presentation;
- artificial scarcity, expiring purchases, fear of missing out, and opaque
  currencies;
- streaks, notifications, energy systems, or social obligation that punish
  healthy absence;
- pay-to-win, pay-to-skip-friction, and accessibility features sold as relief;
- spending by children or people unable to understand odds and total cost;
- matchmaking or difficulty tuned to induce spending rather than support play;
- retention metrics optimized against sleep, work, relationships, or welfare.

State odds, prices, ownership, expiration, and refund boundaries clearly.
Provide spending limits, cooldowns, parental controls, self-exclusion, and
account deletion where relevant. Prefer value people knowingly choose over
compulsion engineered through uncertainty or social pressure.

Do not call a system ethical because it is legally permitted.

## Protect privacy and research participants

Collect only data needed for a named purpose. Define:

- fields, sources, inferences, and sensitive categories;
- consent or other legitimate basis;
- access, security, retention, deletion, export, and breach response;
- whether data affects matchmaking, moderation, personalization, research,
  advertising, employment, education, or public identity;
- how recordings, chat, location, biometrics, and inferred emotion are handled;
- what happens to data when a participant withdraws or the service ends.

Keep research judgments separate from player-facing game state unless the game
explicitly and ethically requires them. Do not infer deception, emotion,
disability, vulnerability, or intent from convenient telemetry without a valid
model and appropriate consent.

For playtests, protect the right to decline, stop, omit questions, and request
deletion. Separate compensation from pressure to complete. Avoid recruiting
dependent participants where refusal could affect work, education, care, or
community standing.

## Treat representation and culture as design

Inspect which people and practices become:

- playable subjects with agency;
- obstacles, resources, rewards, victims, scenery, or jokes;
- mechanically essential stereotypes;
- absent from histories or futures the game claims to represent;
- exposed to player reenactment, appropriation, or harassment;
- responsible for teaching others why the portrayal harms.

Research beyond surface signifiers. Involve relevant expertise early and
compensate it. Do not treat one consultant as permission from an entire group.
Preserve room for difficult, critical, or historically specific work; require
its mechanics, framing, audience, and care to support the intent.

Distinguish depicting harm from rewarding, trivializing, or requiring players to
reproduce it without meaningful context or choice.

## Verify current high-stakes requirements

Treat legal duties, safeguarding standards, clinical claims, physical safety,
gambling classification, consumer protection, privacy, employment, education,
and human-subject research as jurisdiction- and context-dependent. Before giving
high-stakes operational guidance:

1. identify the jurisdictions, ages, setting, data practices, payment model, and
   responsible organization;
2. browse current primary authorities such as regulators, statutes, standards
   bodies, institutional review bodies, and official safeguarding guidance;
3. distinguish mandatory requirements from industry guidance and design advice;
4. cite the current sources used and state what remains outside game-design
   expertise; and
5. obtain qualified legal, safeguarding, medical, security, or research-ethics
   review where the decision requires it.

Do not treat the links in this skill as a complete or current compliance set.
Use them as starting points for design questions and verify live requirements at
the time of the task.

## Run a proportional review

Use a light pass for a low-stakes private puzzle and a formal plan for public,
commercial, embodied, intimate, location-aware, persistent, child-facing, or
high-intensity play.

Report:

1. intended experience and participants;
2. load-bearing access requirements;
3. material hazards and affected people;
4. prevention before mitigation;
5. consent, calibration, exit, and response paths;
6. privacy and economic incentives;
7. unresolved risks and responsible owner; and
8. the representative test still required.

If a risk requires legal, medical, safeguarding, security, or clinical
expertise, name the boundary and obtain it. Do not improvise professional
assurance from a design checklist.

## Selected resources

- [Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/) —
  use its categorized guidance to generate design and test questions.
- [Xbox Accessibility Guidelines](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/guidelines)
  — use platform-oriented criteria and testing considerations for digital games.
- Maury Brown, [Safety and Calibration Design Tools and Their
  Uses](https://www.nordiclarp.org/2018/01/24/safety-calibration-design-tools-uses/)
  — adapt calibration to the actual game and community.
- U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, [The Belmont
  Report](https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/belmont-report/index.html)
  — use as a foundational human-subject research ethics source, then verify the
  applicable institution and jurisdiction.
- NIST, [Privacy Framework](https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework) — use to
  structure privacy-risk questions; verify applicable privacy law separately.
- U.S. Federal Trade Commission, [Bringing Dark Patterns to
  Light](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light) — use as a
  consumer-protection starting point for manipulative interface and monetization
  analysis; verify current local regulation.
