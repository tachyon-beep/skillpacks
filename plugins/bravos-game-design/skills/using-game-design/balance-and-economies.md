# Balance and Economies

Use this reference to evaluate incentives, strategic viability, resource flows,
randomness, tempo, player-count scaling, and multiplayer pathologies. Balance
for the promised experience, not for numerical symmetry alone.

## Contents

- Define the balance target
- Map incentives and strategies
- Model economies
- Manage feedback and tempo
- Use uncertainty and randomness
- Check multiplayer pathologies
- Scale player counts
- Build a balance model
- Simulate selectively

## Define what balance means here

Choose the relevant target:

- **Strategic viability:** several plans can succeed under reachable conditions.
- **Tactical counterplay:** players can recognize and respond to threats without
  one answer becoming compulsory.
- **Opportunity fairness:** differences arise from chosen asymmetry, skill,
  uncertainty, or recoverable variance rather than arbitrary exclusion.
- **Role contribution:** asymmetric roles can affect outcomes and receive
  meaningful attention without identical power.
- **Pacing balance:** advantages, setbacks, and resources transform the session
  at the intended rate.
- **Experiential balance:** pressure, agency, spotlight, vulnerability, and
  cognitive load fit the promise for each participant.
- **Competitive integrity:** outcomes sufficiently reflect the skills and risks
  the game claims to test.

Do not require a dramatic, cooperative, comic, or asymmetric game to produce
equal win rates in every local configuration. Do require the asymmetry to be
legible, intentional, and compatible with consent and the player contract.

## Map incentives before tuning numbers

For each player or role, write:

```text
objective -> valued state -> available actions -> costs and risks
          -> information -> likely responses -> delayed consequence
```

Then ask:

- What behavior does the reward structure make rational?
- What behavior does the fiction or social contract imply instead?
- Can a player improve their outcome by making play worse for everyone?
- Does helping the group sacrifice all personal agency or vice versa?
- Are points, victory, survival, narrative authority, spotlight, and social
  approval pulling in compatible directions?
- Can a player who cannot win still determine who does?
- Is stalling, collusion, sandbagging, farming, griefing, or intentional failure
  rewarded?

Treat incentives as broader than points. Attention, secrecy, authorship,
prestige, safety, revenge, completion, and not disappointing friends all affect
play.

### Test dominance

Call an option locally dominated when another available option is at least as
good across the player's relevant beliefs and strictly better somewhere.
Distinguish:

- an option dominated in one state from a universally weak strategy;
- a strategy that is strong from one skill level or metagame;
- a necessary response to a visible threat from an always-correct move;
- a learning trap from arbitrary false choice.

Use counterfactual states, opponents, player aims, and information. Do not infer
global balance from one play trace.

### Preserve viable differentiation

Prefer options with different strengths, dependencies, timing, risk profiles,
information demands, or expressive identities. If two options do the same job,
keep the clearer one unless redundancy supports resilience, bluffing, drafting,
or player expression.

Avoid balancing every strength by adding an unrelated weakness. Make trade-offs
causally legible: speed versus control, information versus commitment, reach
versus exposure, flexibility versus efficiency.

## Model the economy

Treat an economy as any system of constrained value, including actions, cards,
time, space, trust, attention, health, influence, narrative authority, or
physical stamina.

Map:

- **Stocks:** quantities or states that persist.
- **Sources:** how value enters.
- **Sinks:** how value leaves permanently or temporarily.
- **Converters:** how one value becomes another.
- **Transfers:** how value moves between players or systems.
- **Caps and floors:** hard, soft, spatial, temporal, or social limits.
- **Prices:** fixed, negotiated, auctioned, probabilistic, or opportunity costs.
- **Signals:** what indicates value, scarcity, danger, and ownership.
- **Control:** who can change each flow and who bears the consequence.

Check conservation assumptions. A token may be conserved while its strategic
value changes with time, position, information, or combination. Trust and
attention are not fungible currencies merely because a design labels them as
points.

### Find economic failure

Look for:

- sources exceeding sinks until decisions disappear under abundance;
- sinks exceeding sources until one setback makes play nonviable;
- a converter that creates an infinite or compounding loop;
- a terminal-scoring resource whose value neither shapes meaningful choices nor
  fits the promised resolution;
- hoarding that is safer and stronger than using the resource;
- upkeep that creates administration but no changed choice;
- poverty traps where losing capacity also removes recovery decisions;
- endgame accumulation that makes early play irrelevant;
- hidden prices players cannot learn before irreversible loss; and
- one currency flattening otherwise distinct systems into the same calculation.

## Shape feedback and tempo

Separate reinforcing and balancing feedback:

- **Reinforcing feedback** amplifies advantage or disadvantage. Use it for
  escalation, engine building, territorial momentum, or earned mastery, but
  watch for foregone conclusions.
- **Balancing feedback** resists divergence. Use it for resilience, catch-up,
  tension, or repeated contests, but avoid erasing earned choices.

Evaluate a lead through four questions:

1. Can others perceive it?
2. Can they respond individually or collectively?
3. Does the leader face new decisions or merely execute the win?
4. Can the trailing player still make meaningful progress, expression, or
   impact?

Prefer endogenous counterpressure—exposure, stretched logistics, contested
attention, changing incentives—over arbitrary punishment when it fits the
fiction. Use explicit catch-up when clarity and session competitiveness matter
more than simulation.

Track tempo as rate of meaningful state change, not raw action count. A fast
animation can conceal a strategically static game; a slow negotiation can be
dense with changing beliefs and commitments.

## Use uncertainty and randomness deliberately

Decide whether variance should affect setup, available options, action success,
outcome magnitude, opponent knowledge, or content order. Each location changes
authorship differently.

Check:

- Can players price, mitigate, exploit, or choose exposure to the risk?
- Does variance create new decisions or merely invalidate old ones?
- Does the distribution have rare events that dominate expected experience?
- Does averaging emerge within one session or only across many sessions?
- Is the probability legible enough for the intended audience?
- Does hidden randomness masquerade as another player's intent or skill?
- Does input randomness create a puzzle while output randomness erases a plan?

Do not call a system balanced because expected values match. Distribution,
timing, correlation, recovery, and perceived fairness matter.

## Check multiplayer pathologies

### Kingmaking and leader selection

Ask whether a player with little chance of achieving their own goal can choose
the winner. This may be valid diplomacy when relationships, commitments, and
retaliation are the game. It is a pathology when the final choice is arbitrary,
unprepared, or more powerful than prior play.

### Quarterbacking

In cooperation, inspect whether shared information and a single calculable goal
allow the most confident or experienced player to issue instructions. Repair by
creating complementary private information, simultaneous responsibility,
expressive ownership, bounded communication, or multiple legitimate values—not
by merely prohibiting advice.

### Leader bashing and collusion

Check whether attacking the visible leader is always correct, whether apparent
rank is reliable, and whether coalitions have enforceable or meaningful limits.
Collusion cannot be designed away from every competitive game; define what
coordination is inside the contract and what constitutes out-of-game abuse.

### Analysis paralysis and downtime

Estimate branching, evaluation cost, dependency on others' choices, and the
cost of revising a plan. Reduce irrelevant options, clarify state, stage
commitments, parallelize safe decisions, or shorten planning horizons. Do not
use a timer to conceal an unreadable decision unless time pressure is itself the
desired skill.

Count attention, not only turns. A waiting player can remain engaged through
prediction, negotiation, preparation, audience, or consequential reaction. An
active player can still be disengaged while executing a forced procedure.

### Elimination and exclusion

When elimination is intended, compare its dramatic value with remaining wait,
social exclusion, and ability to leave. Provide a meaningful new role or a short
path to closure only when it fits; do not turn eliminated players into unpaid
administrators.

## Scale player counts explicitly

For each supported count, recalculate:

- action and reaction frequency;
- network edges and negotiation complexity;
- shared-resource pressure;
- information volume and memory burden;
- coalition possibilities and majority thresholds;
- physical reach, component supply, bandwidth, and facilitator load;
- session duration and individual spotlight;
- probability of stalemate, runaway lead, or isolated player.

Do not assume adding resources proportionally preserves the game. Player
interaction often scales quadratically, while attention and time do not.
Declare a narrower supported range when the social topology changes materially.

## Build a balance model

Model only the decision the model must inform. Define:

- purpose and decision owner;
- variables, units, ranges, ownership, and authoritative source;
- starting states, player counts, strategies/policies, and scenario boundaries;
- stocks, flows, payoffs, transitions, distributions, and invariants;
- assumptions about rationality, information, skill, coordination, and behavior;
- outputs and thresholds that would change a design decision;
- calibration observations and missing evidence;
- sensitivity ranges and extreme cases;
- phenomena deliberately excluded from the model.

Use equations, tables, simulation, search, or structured scenarios at the lowest
complexity that answers the question. Distinguish parameter uncertainty from
structural uncertainty: more precise numbers cannot repair a missing causal
relationship or an invalid player model.

Validate dimensional consistency, conservation or creation assumptions,
boundary conditions, termination, and reproducibility. Compare at least one
baseline, one plausible extreme, and one adversarial or degenerate policy.

Treat human strategy, perception, trust, fatigue, accessibility, and social
behavior as empirical inputs or unresolved limitations unless a justified model
exists. Do not invent numerical values merely to complete a spreadsheet.

## Simulate selectively

Use calculation or simulation for bounded questions such as:

- probability and distribution of draws or outcomes;
- economy stability and extreme trajectories;
- reachability, deadlocks, and combinatorial coverage;
- simple policy dominance in a formal state space;
- matchmaking or rating behavior; and
- sensitivity to parameter changes.

Do not use synthetic agents or Monte Carlo output to establish human
legibility, bluffing, delight, trust, social safety, emotional pacing, or the
value of an unconventional performance. Validate the model assumptions before
trusting the precision of its output.

## Finish the balance pass

Report:

1. the intended balance target;
2. the strongest exploit or degeneracy;
3. the states or player counts actually examined;
4. the evidence versus model assumptions;
5. the smallest structural repair before numerical tuning; and
6. the test that would reveal whether the repair worked.
