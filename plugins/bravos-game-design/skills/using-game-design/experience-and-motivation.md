# Experience and Motivation

Use this reference to define what the game is for before inventing rules. Treat
experience models as lenses that generate hypotheses, not formulas that certify
fun.

- Experience palette

- Motivation lenses

## Write an experience thesis

Use this form:

> For **[specific players]** in **[specific context]**, play should create
> **[experience]** through **[player behavior or relationship]**. Expect to see
> **[observable signs]**. Treat **[counter-observation]** as evidence against the
> current design.

Prefer “friends build precarious trust by making commitments whose costs become
visible later” to “a fun diplomacy game.” Name what players do, not only what the
designer hopes they feel.

Add an anti-goal when adjacent experiences would violate the promise:

> Preserve tense uncertainty; avoid humiliation, arbitrary unknowability, and
> compulsory deception.

Allow several compatible experiences, but identify the primary one. A game that
tries to maximize mastery, surprise, relaxation, dramatic authorship, social
equality, and ruthless competition simultaneously usually hides unresolved
trade-offs.

## Use an experience palette

Consider, without treating this list as exhaustive:

- **Mastery:** learn, practice, execute, optimize, or improvise with growing
  competence.
- **Agency and expression:** make choices that feel authored and reveal a style,
  value, identity, or strategy.
- **Discovery:** form and test models, find secrets, notice patterns, or explore
  a possibility space.
- **Tension and relief:** anticipate consequential uncertainty, then receive
  release, reversal, or settlement.
- **Fellowship and belonging:** coordinate, recognize, support, joke with, or
  care for other people.
- **Competition and status:** compare skill, read rivals, take risks, and earn a
  legible result.
- **Stewardship and care:** nurture a person, place, system, collection, or shared
  future.
- **Fantasy and role:** inhabit capabilities, responsibilities, identities, or
  perspectives unavailable in ordinary life.
- **Narrative and meaning:** cause, interpret, or witness consequential change.
- **Creation:** make artifacts, performances, strategies, spaces, or stories
  that remain recognizably one's own.
- **Comedy and social chaos:** create permission for surprise, incongruity,
  escalation, and shared recovery.
- **Sensory and kinesthetic pleasure:** move, manipulate, time, aim, arrange, or
  inhabit space satisfyingly.
- **Contemplation:** attend, reflect, notice, or make meaning without constant
  pressure.
- **Chosen discomfort:** explore grief, complicity, horror, frustration,
  vulnerability, or moral uncertainty with informed consent and adequate care.

Do not equate fun only with pleasure. A successful game may be moving,
exhausting, tragic, difficult, or deliberately unreplayable. Require the
experience to be chosen, supported, and coherent with the promise.

## Model players and context

Design for people in a situation, not for an abstract “player.” Check:

- familiarity with games, genre conventions, rules, performance, and one
  another;
- competitive appetite, tolerance for uncertainty, conflict, exposure,
  dexterity, arithmetic, memory, reading, and improvisation;
- sensory, motor, cognitive, linguistic, cultural, and scheduling access;
- power differences, existing relationships, trust, and reasons for attending;
- group size, spectators, drop-in/drop-out behavior, and whether a facilitator
  or host carries hidden labor;
- session duration, repetition, environment, fatigue, interruptions, privacy,
  and consequences outside play.

Segment when needs genuinely differ. Do not average incompatible audiences into
a fictional universal player.

## Use motivation lenses carefully

### Autonomy, competence, and relatedness

Use self-determination theory to ask:

- **Autonomy:** Can players endorse and author their actions, or are rewards and
  constraints merely steering them?
- **Competence:** Can players form a model, improve, and perceive the connection
  between effort and outcome?
- **Relatedness:** Does play create meaningful recognition or connection, not
  merely proximity or a chat channel?

Do not require every game to maximize all three. A constrained tragedy may
deliberately narrow autonomy; a solitary puzzle may make relatedness irrelevant.
Use the lens to expose trade-offs and mismatches.

### Intrinsic and extrinsic goals

Distinguish pleasure in the activity from pursuit of points, unlocks, status,
completion, prizes, attendance, or obligation. Extrinsic structure can focus
attention and make progress legible, but can also crowd out curiosity, care, or
experimentation.

Ask what players would still choose if the badge, streak, loot, leaderboard, or
metagame reward disappeared. If the answer is “nothing,” inspect the activity
before improving the reward schedule.

### Challenge and learning

Match challenge to the model players can actually form. Difficulty without
legible causality produces helplessness; clarity without live alternatives
produces routine. Provide feedback soon enough to learn, but preserve ambiguity
when interpretation is the intended play.

## Translate experience into behavior

Move through four levels:

1. **Experience:** “uneasy cooperation.”
2. **Behavior:** players share partial plans, accept dependencies, and watch for
   defection.
3. **Dynamic:** commitments create mutual benefit now and asymmetric exposure
   later.
4. **Mechanic:** simultaneous allocation plus a delayed, publicly attributable
   cost for abandoned commitments.

Generate more than one dynamic for the same experience. “Tension” might arise
from time pressure, hidden information, irreversible commitment, social
exposure, execution difficulty, spatial encroachment, or scarce opportunity.
Those routes create different games and different risks.

Use this translation table as prompts:

| Intended experience | Look for behavior such as | Candidate causal levers |
|---|---|---|
| Mastery | predicts, executes, adapts, teaches | stable rules, graded feedback, expressive action space |
| Discovery | hypothesizes, experiments, revises | hidden regularity, safe probes, revealing consequences |
| Trust | commits, relies, verifies, forgives | dependency, costly signals, incomplete control, memory |
| Tension | hesitates, forecasts, watches, commits | stakes, uncertainty, deadlines, irreversibility |
| Expression | chooses recognizably different styles | open combinatorics, ownership, visible consequence |
| Fellowship | coordinates, celebrates, repairs | shared goals, complementary roles, mutual recognition |
| Comedy | sets up, escalates, surprises, recovers | permissive constraints, reversible failure, audience |
| Contemplation | notices, interprets, reflects | space, low urgency, ambiguity, persistent traces |

## Set evidence and failure conditions

Choose observations appropriate to the claim:

- Use choice traces and counterfactuals for agency and strategic sensitivity.
- Use error patterns and explanation for legibility and learning.
- Use conversation topology for coordination, negotiation, or dominance.
- Use return behavior cautiously for durable value; control for obligation,
  rewards, and social pressure.
- Use self-report for subjective experience, while accounting for politeness,
  demand characteristics, memory, and vocabulary.
- Use physiological or telemetry signals only when the inference from signal to
  experience is justified.

Precommit a failure sentence. Examples:

- “Players identify the intended choice but regard its consequences as
  cosmetic.”
- “One participant supplies every plan while others execute instructions.”
- “Surprise comes from unavailable information rather than missed evidence.”
- “Players understand the system yet do not care which outcome occurs.”

Do not ask only “Was it fun?” Ask for remembered decisions, moments of changed
intent, expectations, surprises, frustrations, and desired next actions.

## Avoid common traps

- Designing for the designer's literacy and calling confusion depth.
- Treating a motivation taxonomy as a list of features to include.
- Confusing arousal with enjoyment, retention with welfare, or compliance with
  voluntary engagement.
- Assuming novelty will survive repetition.
- Assuming all competition creates tension or all cooperation creates
  fellowship.
- Adding rewards when the activity lacks interesting decisions.
- Ignoring non-acting players, spectators, facilitators, and eliminated players.
- Universalizing one playtest group's culture or relationship norms.

## Selected foundations

- Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek, [MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game
  Research](https://www.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf) — use the
  mechanics/dynamics/aesthetics separation as a translation lens.
- Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski, [The Motivational Pull of Video Games: A
  Self-Determination Theory Approach](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2006_RyanRigbyPrzybylski_MandE.pdf)
  — use autonomy, competence, and relatedness as testable motivational
  constructs, not a complete theory of game value.
