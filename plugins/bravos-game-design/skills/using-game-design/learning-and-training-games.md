# Learning and Training Games

Use this reference when a promised outcome includes knowledge, skill,
decision-making, transfer, assessment, or changed real-world performance. Do not
infer learning from engagement or in-game success alone.

## Contents

- Define the learning claim
- Model learners and prior knowledge
- Align mechanics with practice
- Design feedback and adaptation
- Validate assessment
- Test retention and transfer
- Protect learners

## Define the learning claim

Specify:

> Given **[conditions and supports]**, **[learners]** will perform **[observable
> knowledge, skill, judgment, or behavior]** to **[criterion]** in **[game and
> transfer context]**, retained for **[relevant interval]**.

Distinguish:

- exposure to content;
- recall or recognition;
- conceptual understanding;
- procedural fluency;
- discrimination between cases;
- judgment under uncertainty;
- collaboration or communication;
- physical or perceptual performance;
- near transfer to a similar context;
- far transfer to materially different conditions;
- real-world behavior and outcome.

Do not use “raise awareness,” “understand,” or “learn” without an observable
performance and context. Separate the learning objective from engagement,
completion, score, enjoyment, and willingness to continue.

Name anti-objectives: misconceptions, unsafe shortcuts, rote strategies that
fail outside the game, false confidence, or incentives to game the assessment.

## Model learners and prior knowledge

Check:

- existing knowledge, misconceptions, language, culture, literacy, numeracy,
  motor skill, technology access, and domain experience;
- motivation to learn versus obligation, credential, employment, school, or
  compliance pressure;
- whether collaboration hides individual gaps or supports genuine explanation;
- prerequisite concepts and skills needed before the first useful practice;
- how experts and novices perceive different information in the same state;
- consequences of error in the game and in the transfer environment;
- accessibility accommodations that must not invalidate the intended construct.

Design diagnostics that change support rather than label identity. Do not
confuse familiarity with games, speed, reading load, or confidence with mastery
of the target domain.

## Align mechanics with the learning process

Map every learning objective to what players repeatedly perceive, retrieve,
decide, perform, explain, and receive feedback about.

| Learning objective | Game action/practice | Feedback | Variation | Transfer bridge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

Useful practice structures include:

- retrieval from memory rather than repeated exposure;
- discrimination among confusable cases;
- generation, prediction, or explanation before reveal;
- worked examples followed by faded support;
- repeated practice spaced across contexts and time;
- immediate correction for foundational procedure;
- delayed or consequence-based feedback for integrated judgment;
- scenario variation that preserves deep structure while changing surface cues;
- reflection that connects an in-game choice to the domain model;
- collaborative explanation with individual accountability;
- simulation of rare, dangerous, costly, or time-compressed situations;
- safe failure followed by retry with changed strategy.

Do not add points and narrative around a quiz and assume the learning process has
changed. Do not let a powerful general game strategy bypass the target skill.

## Design feedback and adaptation

Make feedback answer the learner's current question:

- Was the action performed correctly?
- Which cue or principle mattered?
- Why did this outcome follow?
- What misconception does the error reveal?
- Which part should be retried, varied, or combined next?
- How certain should the learner be?

Avoid feedback that gives the answer before productive effort or leaves a
misconception uncorrected. Separate motivational presentation from diagnostic
information.

Adapt on evidence tied to the construct. Adjust support, complexity, timing,
scenario variation, or feedback—not only enemy health or point thresholds.
Preserve challenge and dignity; do not trap learners in an opaque low track or
publicly expose remediation.

## Validate assessment

Ask whether the observed score or action represents the intended capability.
Check:

- **Construct representation:** Does success require the knowledge or skill the
  game claims to assess?
- **Construct-irrelevant variance:** Can reading speed, dexterity, device
  familiarity, strategy-game expertise, charisma, guessing, or group help drive
  the result instead?
- **Reliability:** Would comparable performance produce a comparable result?
- **Authenticity:** Does the task preserve the important conditions and cues of
  the transfer setting?
- **Security and coaching:** Can memorized items, collaboration, retries, or
  external tools invalidate the inference?
- **Consequence:** Is the assessment appropriate for low-stakes feedback, or is
  it being used for certification, employment, safety, or access decisions?

Use in-game telemetry as evidence only after mapping each event to the construct
and validating alternative explanations. A high score can reflect exploitation
of the game rather than learning.

For high-stakes assessment, obtain psychometric, domain, accessibility, legal,
and institutional review. A game-design skill cannot certify validity.

## Test retention and transfer

Test at distinct levels:

1. **In-game acquisition:** Can learners improve on trained situations?
2. **Retention:** Does performance persist after a meaningful delay?
3. **Near transfer:** Can learners apply the principle to new examples with the
   same deep structure?
4. **Far or real-world transfer:** Can learners recognize and act under changed
   tools, stakes, distractions, and social conditions?

Use a pretest when prior knowledge varies and the inference needs change scores.
Include a comparison condition when the decision concerns whether the game adds
value beyond another method. Precommit outcomes, missing data, and attrition
handling.

Observe negative transfer: a simplified simulation may teach certainty,
shortcuts, timing, or incentives that become unsafe outside it. Debrief and
bridging prompts help but do not prove transfer.

Do not treat immediate post-play confidence, enjoyment, completion, or ability
to repeat game terminology as retained learning.

## Protect learners

Account for:

- compulsory participation and authority differences;
- humiliation, ranking, stereotype threat, and public failure;
- accessibility and accommodations;
- data used for grading, employment, discipline, profiling, or selection;
- inaccurate feedback and false confidence in safety-critical domains;
- minors, dependent relationships, consent, withdrawal, and privacy;
- monetization or retention incentives conflicting with learner welfare;
- cultural and domain assumptions embedded in scenarios and scoring.

Do not make failure emotionally or socially costly merely to increase intensity.
Separate research participation from required instruction or employment.

## Selected foundations

- National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, [How People Learn
  II](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/24783/how-people-learn-ii-learners-contexts-and-cultures)
  — use its synthesis to frame learners, prior knowledge, context, and learning
  processes.
- Institute of Education Sciences, [What Works Clearinghouse Standards
  Handbook](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Handbooks) — use current standards as a
  starting point when making causal learning-effect claims; obtain appropriate
  research expertise.
