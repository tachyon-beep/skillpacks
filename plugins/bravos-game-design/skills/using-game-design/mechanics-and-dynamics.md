# Mechanics and Dynamics

Use this reference to turn an experiential intent into rules that cause player
behavior. Specify mechanics precisely enough to prototype, while keeping their
causal claims open to test.

## Contents

- Work backward from experience
- Generate distinct candidates
- Meaningful decisions
- Information, uncertainty, and randomness
- Feedback and learning
- Interaction and consequence
- Mechanic card and stress tests

## Specify the mechanic grammar

Describe a mechanic through these parts:

1. **Actor:** who may or must act?
2. **Trigger and timing:** when does the opportunity appear, and who knows it?
3. **Perception:** what state, options, costs, and uncertainty can the actor
   inspect?
4. **Input:** what choice, performance, communication, or commitment occurs?
5. **Procedure:** how does the game resolve it, including human adjudication?
6. **State change:** what capability, resource, information, position,
   relationship, or rule changes?
7. **Feedback:** who learns what, when, and with what fidelity?
8. **Downstream use:** which later decision or performance consumes that change?
9. **Exit and exception:** how does the process stop, fail, tie, or recover?

If a mechanic cannot answer these questions, it is a premise or aesthetic idea,
not yet an executable rule.

Treat procedures implemented by a facilitator, social norm, physics, software,
or player interpretation as real mechanics. Do not count only code or written
rules.

## Work backward from experience

Use this causal chain:

```text
desired experience
  <- recurring player behavior
  <- incentives, information, constraints, and feedback
  <- mechanics and material affordances
```

For example:

```text
experience: precarious trust
behavior: rely on another player while retaining suspicion
dynamic: both benefit from commitment, but later evidence can reveal defection
mechanic: simultaneous commitments, delayed public outcome, costly audit
```

Do not assume the chain is deterministic. Players import motives, relationships,
skills, and interpretations. Phrase the mechanic as a causal hypothesis and
identify what else could produce the same observation.

## Generate causally distinct candidates

Generate two or three candidates by changing the source of the dynamic, not its
surface dressing. To create tension, compare candidates such as:

- **Commitment tension:** make a consequential choice before relevant
  information arrives.
- **Execution tension:** reveal the correct action but demand difficult timing,
  coordination, dexterity, or performance.
- **Interpretive tension:** provide incomplete but meaningful evidence and make
  action reveal a belief.

Other reusable causal levers include:

- scarcity and opportunity cost;
- spatial position, adjacency, reach, blocking, or movement;
- private information and selective revelation;
- simultaneous choice, bidding, bluffing, drafting, or voting;
- asymmetric capability or responsibility;
- push-your-luck escalation and voluntary stopping;
- construction, combination, transformation, and tool use;
- memory, residue, reputation, or persistent world change;
- time pressure, delayed payoff, decay, or windows of opportunity;
- role authority, negotiation, interpretation, or performance;
- physical manipulation, coordination, aim, rhythm, endurance, or balance.

Select levers that fit the medium and audience. Do not import a resource economy
because it is easy to quantify.

## Build meaningful decisions

Require more than option count. Check:

- **Live alternatives:** at least two options can be rational under reachable
  states or distinct player aims.
- **Perceived distinction:** the actor can recognize a relevant difference,
  even if outcomes remain uncertain.
- **Trade-off:** options sacrifice different values, timing, information,
  relationships, or future possibilities.
- **Ownership:** the outcome feels connected to the actor's judgment or
  performance rather than arbitrary assignment.
- **Consequence:** the choice changes later action, information, incentive,
  capability, relationship, or interpretation.
- **Recoverability fit:** reversibility or irreversibility matches the intended
  stakes and learning stage.

Allow routine or expressive actions when they serve rhythm, embodiment,
communication, or fantasy. Do not mislabel them as strategic decisions.

Test dominance at the level of beliefs and incentives available to the player,
not only with omniscient calculation. An objectively inferior action can remain
meaningful when discovering that inferiority is intended learning; it becomes a
trap when the lesson is arbitrary, excessively costly, or never legible.

## Design information and uncertainty

Separate:

- **Unknown state:** a fact exists but is hidden or noisy.
- **Unknown outcome:** a known action has probabilistic or opponent-dependent
  consequences.
- **Unknown model:** players have not yet learned how the system behaves.
- **Unknown intent:** another person controls relevant future action.
- **Execution uncertainty:** success depends on skill, timing, or coordination.

Choose what players can infer, not merely what is concealed. Hidden information
without discriminating evidence creates guessing; complete information without
interacting incentives can create calculation rather than play.

Use randomness to:

- vary situations and prevent rote execution;
- create risk that players can price, mitigate, or exploit;
- distribute content or roles fairly;
- generate prompts for interpretation or improvisation; or
- compress a simulation into uncertain outcomes.

Avoid randomness that erases authorship after a demanding decision, hides an
uninteresting system, or creates irreversible loss before meaningful action.
Expose probabilities only when exact knowledge serves the intended play.

## Create feedback for learning and feeling

Match feedback to the question a player needs answered:

- Did the input register?
- Did execution succeed?
- What changed?
- Why did it change?
- What can be inferred but not known?
- What new action is available?
- How close is an objective, danger, or transformation?

Separate immediate input feedback from delayed strategic consequence. Preserve
ambiguity deliberately, not accidentally. If interpretation is play, reveal
enough stable evidence to support competing models and later learning.

Use presentation as part of the mechanic. Component layout, animation, sound,
spatial arrangement, facilitator cadence, and ritual can make timing,
ownership, stakes, and consequence legible. A rule that exists only in the
designer's explanation is not functioning at the table.

## Connect players and consequences

Map every action to affected players:

| Person | Immediate effect | Information gained | Later option changed | Social meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Actor | | | | |
| Target or partner | | | | |
| Other active players | | | | |
| Waiting, eliminated, or observing players | | | | |
| Facilitator or host | | | | |

Look for second-order effects: one player's action changes another player's
state, which changes a third player's incentives. These chains often create
global coherence, but excessive coupling can make outcomes unreadable.

Do not force direct conflict into every game. Interaction may arise through
shared resources, prediction, observation, imitation, negotiation, timing,
joint construction, audience, comparison, or changes to a common environment.

Keep tunable values explicit, but do not tune before the causal job works.

## Stress-test the mechanic

Run these thought experiments before prototyping:

- **Removal:** What disappears if the mechanic is removed?
- **Always/never:** What happens if a player always uses it or never does?
- **Extremes:** What happens at minimum and maximum player count, resources,
  skill, and session length?
- **Information:** What changes if relevant state is public, private, delayed,
  or noisy?
- **Collusion and hostility:** Can players coordinate, stall, grief, kingmake,
  or target someone outside the intended contract?
- **Learning:** Can a player connect action to outcome soon enough to improve?
- **Administration:** Who performs the bookkeeping, adjudication, setup, or
  emotional labor?
- **Translation:** Would another medium automate, expose, or invalidate its
  intended dynamic?

Use the results to decide what the prototype must exercise. Do not mistake a
thought experiment for evidence of the human experience.
