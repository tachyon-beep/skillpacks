# Tabletop and Facilitated Media

Use this reference for board and card games, tabletop roleplaying games, and
other forms where people manipulate shared materials or adjudicate open action.

## Contents

- Board and card affordances and costs
- Components, teaching, and administration
- Tabletop roleplaying and facilitated play
- Authority, preparation, and campaign continuity

## Use board and card affordances

Exploit:

- shared visible state and social co-presence;
- tactile manipulation, spatial arrangement, ritual, and table talk;
- flexible human enforcement and recoverable house rulings;
- finite components that make possibility and scarcity tangible;
- easy modification for rapid prototypes.

Pay for:

- setup, teardown, storage, shuffling, sorting, counting, and component cost;
- manual resolution and error propagation;
- hidden-information handling and accidental leaks;
- table reach, visual density, lighting, color, text size, dexterity, and space;
- teach burden, rules lookup, ownership ambiguity, and downtime;
- different social topologies at different player counts.

Check:

- Can players read actionable state from their normal seat?
- Who maintains each track, deck, bag, token pool, and phase transition?
- Does physical procedure create anticipation, ownership, or communication?
- Can components encode rules instead of requiring memory?
- What happens when a piece is missing, bumped, miscounted, or revealed?
- Can the game be taught in the order players need concepts?
- Does simultaneous handling create collisions or useful energy?
- Does the box promise match setup and session reality?

Use component limits to clarify the design. Do not add a deck, track, token type,
or board region without a distinct information or interaction job.

## Design components, teaching, and administration

Create a component grammar: distinguish ownership, type, state, value, timing,
and legal placement through more than color alone. Keep public state facing the
people who use it. Let component state remove rules lookup where practical.

Order teaching by the first decisions players must make:

1. promise and objective;
2. visible state and ownership;
3. first legal actions and immediate feedback;
4. consequence consumed by the next decision;
5. exceptions and later systems when they become relevant.

Run a blind setup and teach. Record every hidden step, component search, rules
lookup, and facilitator patch. Budget administration as shared attention, not
free labor.

## Use tabletop roleplaying and facilitated affordances

Exploit:

- human interpretation, improvisation, and responsiveness;
- broad action possibility and fictional positioning;
- shared authorship, character identification, and persistent campaigns;
- a facilitator's ability to pace, frame, adjudicate, and tailor;
- conversation as high-bandwidth input and output.

Pay for:

- preparation, facilitation skill, judgment consistency, and emotional labor;
- uneven spotlight, confidence, rules mastery, and authority;
- ambiguous expectations about winning, advocacy, authorship, and tone;
- memory and continuity across sessions;
- social pressure to accept rulings or perform.

Check:

- Who may establish facts, frame adversity, and settle ambiguity?
- What does a player decide as a player versus as a character?
- Do resolution rules create consequences or only answer success/failure?
- Can every role initiate play and alter direction?
- How are spotlight and interruption managed?
- What happens when facilitator preparation or improvisation fails?
- Do advancement and campaign persistence transform decisions or only numbers?
- Are safety, attendance, replacement, and character exit supported?

Write procedures around recurring high-cost judgments. Preserve open judgment
where interpretation is the point.

## Make authority and preparation visible

Define who controls facts, framing, adversity, characters, pacing, rules,
secrets, and safety. Give human judgment principles and appeal/exit routes.

Budget preparation by purpose:

- situations and pressures that create decisions;
- characters, places, factions, and evidence players can affect;
- procedures for consequences, clocks, travel, downtime, and recovery;
- reference material the facilitator can retrieve during play;
- contingencies for missing participants and abandoned directions.

Do not require a prewritten plot to survive contact with player action. Do not
require a facilitator to improvise every load-bearing consequence. Preserve
campaign memory in the states and relationships later sessions actually
consume, not only in recap prose or growing numbers.
