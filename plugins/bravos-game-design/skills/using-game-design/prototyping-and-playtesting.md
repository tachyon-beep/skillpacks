# Prototyping and Playtesting

Use this reference to choose the cheapest artifact that can answer the current
design question, run a playtest that produces interpretable evidence, and make
an iteration decision without laundering enthusiasm into proof.

## Contents

- Order the risks
- Write a testable claim
- Choose prototype fidelity
- Select a playtest type
- Run the session
- Observe and debrief
- Interpret evidence
- Decide and iterate

## Order the risks

Test the most existential uncertain claim before polishing downstream details.
A useful default order is:

1. **Core viability:** Does the central form create the participation it
   promises? Test live alternatives and downstream consequences when agency is
   the promise; test expressive execution, learning, ritual transformation,
   sensory response, social coordination, discovery, or another explicit
   participation claim when those are the point. Do not reject chance-led,
   contemplative, performative, or self-contained play for lacking strategic
   decisions it never promised.
2. **Legibility and usability:** Can intended players perceive state, operate the
   interface or procedure, and form a model sufficient to act?
3. **Experiential payoff:** Does play create the intended emotion, relationship,
   fantasy, expression, or meaning?
4. **Strategic and social robustness:** Do viable alternatives, counterplay,
   pacing, safety, and player-count behavior survive variation?
5. **Content and longevity:** Does repetition transform play enough for the
   intended duration or replay promise?
6. **Production and polish:** Does final presentation improve the working
   experience without hiding structural failure?

Change the order when a safety, access, technical, or logistical feasibility
risk can invalidate the form before gamehood testing.

Do not use a lower gate to accept a higher one. A mechanically complete trace
cannot prove human delight; laughter cannot prove strategic viability; return
behavior cannot by itself prove welfare or accessibility. Treat this ordering
as a risk heuristic, not a universal definition of games.

## Write a testable claim

Use:

```markdown
**Claim:** [Specific players] will [observable behavior] because [mechanical
cause], producing [intended experience or design consequence].

**Support:** We would observe [pattern].
**Contradiction:** We would observe [pattern].
**Inconclusive:** The test cannot distinguish the claim when [confound or missing
condition].
**Decision:** If supported, [next step]. If contradicted, [repair or removal].
```

Examples:

- “Teams will voluntarily expose partial plans because complementary private
  information makes coordination more valuable than secrecy.”
- “Players will feel mounting responsibility because every shortcut leaves a
  persistent artifact that constrains a later choice.”
- “The dexterity action will create expressive mastery rather than exclusion
  when speed is optional and accuracy has multiple viable techniques.”

Avoid claims such as “players will have fun” or “the mechanic works.” Name the
behavior and causal theory.

## Choose the minimum valid prototype

Match fidelity to the uncertainty:

| Question | Useful prototype |
|---|---|
| Are there meaningful decisions? | paper state machine, decision cards, rules trace |
| Does information change choices? | mocked states, controlled reveal, paired conditions |
| Is physical performance satisfying? | safe material mockup at representative scale |
| Does negotiation emerge? | facilitated slice with real incentives and consequences |
| Is a digital control expressive? | input/feedback toy before content or progression |
| Does a session arc transform? | compressed multi-cycle run, not one isolated turn |
| Can players learn unaided? | blind rules/interface test |
| Is intense live play supportable? | calibrated workshop and scene fragment before event |

Build only enough surrounding structure to keep the tested dynamic authentic.
Too little context tests a different interaction; too much fidelity introduces
cost, attachment, and confounds.

Label substitutions. If a facilitator secretly resolves incomplete rules, the
test includes human judgment. If tokens stand in for physical pursuit, the test
does not establish bodily access or risk. If an LLM stands in for a player, the
test does not establish human interpretation or enjoyment.

## Select the playtest type

- **Designer trace:** execute rules literally to find missing states, deadlocks,
  ambiguous timing, and obvious dominance.
- **Internal playtest:** let informed collaborators challenge the structure
  quickly; expect explanation and shared assumptions to contaminate usability
  evidence.
- **Moderated target-player test:** observe intended players while controlling
  the scenario and recording decisions.
- **Blind test:** provide only the intended product, rules, briefing, or
  facilitator materials. Use it for learnability, handoff, and hidden labor.
- **Comparative or ablation test:** change one relevant mechanic or information
  channel while holding the rest as equivalent as practical.
- **Longitudinal test:** observe learning, metagame, fatigue, progression,
  community behavior, and persistence across sessions.
- **Stress and edge test:** exercise extreme counts, hostile strategies,
  accessibility modes, component failures, dropout, reconnect, and safety
  procedures.
- **Workshop or calibration test:** rehearse intense social or embodied
  interactions without requiring full character investment or event duration.

Use qualitative tests to discover causes and quantitative tests to estimate
patterns only after measures and sampling support the inference. Small samples
can reject brittle assumptions; they cannot establish broad preferences or
population rates.

## Prepare the protocol

Record before the session:

- claim, support, contradiction, and inconclusive conditions;
- target participants and why they represent the intended context;
- recruitment, consent, compensation, privacy, withdrawal, and follow-up;
- exact rules, build, scenario, components, facilitator script, and known
  deviations;
- session phases, tasks, prompts, data captured, and who captures it;
- interventions permitted for rules errors, distress, technical failure, or
  deadlock;
- stopping conditions and safety escalation;
- analysis method and decision owner.

Do not silently repair the prototype mid-test. Mark the intervention, its time,
and the affected evidence. Run a new condition when the change matters.

For comparative conditions, hold stable the player population, incentives,
starting state, information, duration, facilitation, and presentation unless a
difference is part of the hypothesis. Record order and learning effects.

## Observe the session

Capture behavior before interpretation:

- legal options available and selected;
- time spent, revisions, passes, requests, errors, and abandoned actions;
- information inspected or ignored;
- predictions and commitments made before outcomes;
- state, capability, relationship, and later-choice changes;
- who initiates, proposes, interrupts, decides, waits, withdraws, or facilitates;
- rule explanations and interventions;
- visible access, fatigue, safety, technical, and logistical failures;
- exact moments relevant to the precommitted claim.

Use timestamps, state snapshots, photographs, recordings, or telemetry only with
appropriate consent and data boundaries. A concise structured log is often more
useful than a complete transcript.

Do not code intent, emotion, deception, or causality as objective fact. Record
the action and later ask or infer with an explicit basis.

## Debrief without leading

Ask for reconstruction before evaluation:

- What were you trying to make happen at this point?
- Which options did you seriously consider, and what distinguished them?
- What did you expect would happen?
- When did your plan or interpretation change, and why?
- What information did you use or wish you had?
- Which consequence felt attributable to your action?
- Where did you wait, disengage, feel pressured, or lose the model?
- What would you try next if play continued?

Then ask about subjective experience in the participant's language. Avoid
forcing the designer's vocabulary: a player may describe “careful,” “awkward,”
or “locked in” more accurately than choosing from a supplied fun taxonomy.

Separate research debrief from safety check-out. Never require public disclosure
of distress or personal history as feedback.

## Interpret evidence honestly

Build an evidence table:

| Claim | Observation | Participant report | Inference | Alternative explanation | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Watch for:

- facilitator explanation making a weak signal legible;
- novelty creating energy that repetition may not sustain;
- politeness, friendship, compensation, or obligation inflating praise;
- one charismatic participant supplying the intended dynamic;
- rules errors accidentally improving or harming play;
- experienced players solving onboarding for others;
- a metric moving for a different reason than intended;
- survivorship when excluded or dissatisfied players leave;
- strong moments masking long periods of waiting;
- aggregate success hiding one seat's lack of agency.

Treat “players did not use it” as ambiguous. They may not have noticed it,
understood it, valued it, needed it in the tested state, trusted its consequence,
or had permission to act. Design a discriminating probe before deleting or
buffing it.

## Use synthetic and analytical tests within bounds

Use rules traces, search, simulation, and synthetic agents to test:

- legal-action and state coverage;
- deterministic resolution, termination, and replay;
- reachability, invariants, and deadlocks;
- bounded dominance and economy sensitivity;
- hidden-information leaks; and
- whether prior state can change a later formal choice.

Do not use them to accept claims about human fun, comprehension, salience,
social trust, performance, bleed, safety, accessibility, or return desire.
Model fluency and explanation are not player evidence.

## Make the iteration decision

Choose:

- **Keep** when the causal claim survives at the tested scope.
- **Rewire** when the local interaction works but does not propagate or produces
  the wrong downstream dynamic.
- **Simplify** when a lower-cost rule preserves the observed job.
- **Shelve** when the idea has value but not in this game's current spine.
- **Kill** when the premise conflicts with protected intent, welfare, or
  feasibility.
- **Retest unchanged** when the result is genuinely inconclusive and a
  corrected test can discriminate it.

Change one causal assumption at a time where practical. Preserve version,
rules, scenario, and evidence lineage. Do not convert every observation into a
feature request.

Finish with the next riskiest uncertainty, not the most visible polish issue.
