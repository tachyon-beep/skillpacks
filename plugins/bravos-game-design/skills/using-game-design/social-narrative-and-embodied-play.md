# Social, Narrative, and Embodied Play

Use this reference when the game relies on relationships, communication,
performance, facilitation, fiction, physical space, touch, intensity, or live
co-presence. Treat social norms, human adjudication, and the body as parts of the
system rather than external decoration.

## Contents

- Social contract and topology
- Cooperation, competition, and negotiation
- Authority and facilitation
- Narrative causality

## Design the social contract

State what participants are agreeing to do to and for one another. Include:

- whether winning, dramatic play, character advocacy, group success, discovery,
  performance, or care takes priority when they conflict;
- permitted deception, secrecy, persuasion, interruption, physicality,
  targeting, exclusion, romance, intimacy, horror, and conflict;
- how to pause, calibrate, withdraw, or leave without needing to justify it;
- what information remains private before, during, and after play;
- the responsibilities of players, facilitators, hosts, performers, audience,
  safety staff, and organizers; and
- what behavior is outside the game even when it would help someone win.

Do not rely on genre literacy or friendship to make the contract implicit.
Existing groups contain power differences and unspoken histories; strangers
contain uncertainty and unequal comfort with refusing.

## Map the social topology

For each phase, map who can address, affect, observe, interrupt, exclude, or
depend on whom. Check:

- central participants who receive disproportionate information or attention;
- peripheral participants with no necessary relationship;
- brokers who can control communication between groups;
- public versus private channels and the cost of switching;
- whether a facilitator becomes the only source of stakes or permission;
- whether audience attention rewards escalation beyond the intended boundary;
- whether a role's apparent power differs from its actual social exposure.

Social interaction does not arise from placing people in a room. Give them
reasons to attend to, need, interpret, affect, and recognize one another.

## Shape cooperation and competition

### Cooperation

Create interdependence without erasing ownership. Use complementary capability,
partial knowledge, simultaneous responsibility, joint creation, negotiated
priorities, or individual stewardship. Let each participant make a contribution
that others cannot simply dictate.

Inspect whether failure belongs to the team while blame lands on the least
experienced player. Provide recovery and learning that do not turn one mistake
into social punishment.

### Competition

Make the contested value and permitted opposition clear. Preserve counterplay,
recognizable authorship, and a reason to remain engaged when behind. Separate
in-game rivalry from permission to humiliate, harass, deceive outside declared
channels, or exploit real vulnerability.

### Negotiation and trust

Give statements mechanical relevance through the commitments, information,
capabilities, and relationships they concern—not necessarily by assigning
semantic truth values to speech.

Useful structures include:

- costly or constrained commitments;
- selective information and verifiable fragments;
- dependencies whose benefit and exposure appear at different times;
- records, reputation, residue, or witnesses;
- enforceable, breakable, or deliberately informal agreements;
- shared threats with conflicting preferred responses; and
- actions that reveal priorities without fully revealing intent.

Avoid “negotiation” where one numerical exchange rate makes every agreement
obvious. Avoid trust systems where a score replaces the need to interpret
behavior unless reputation management is the intended play.

## Make authority explicit

Treat authority as a mechanic. Distinguish who may:

- state facts about the world;
- interpret ambiguous outcomes;
- frame scenes or introduce adversity;
- control a character, faction, prop, location, or shared resource;
- pace, pause, rewind, or end play;
- resolve disputes and enforce boundaries; and
- modify rules during the session.

For facilitated games, budget preparation, attention, memory, emotional labor,
and improvisation. A design that works only when an expert facilitator silently
repairs it has not externalized its actual rules.

Use procedures when consistency and trust matter; use judgment when
responsiveness and interpretation matter. State the principles that guide
judgment and give participants a way to challenge or exit it.

## Connect mechanics and narrative

Choose the narrative relationship deliberately:

- **Authored sequence:** mechanics pace, gate, vary, or embody prepared events.
- **Branching consequence:** choices select or transform authored material.
- **Emergent narrative:** persistent agents, systems, relationships, and traces
  create events that players interpret as story.
- **Player-authored narrative:** prompts, roles, authority, and constraints let
  participants create the content directly.
- **Interpretive work:** the game presents fragments or experiences whose
  meaning players construct.

Require mechanical consequence when the game promises authorship. A dialogue
choice that changes only the next line may support characterization, but do not
call it world-shaping agency.

Preserve causal memory. Let later play acknowledge earlier relationships,
commitments, losses, discoveries, and creations. Avoid a story generator whose
events are vivid but interchangeable because nothing persists.

Use uncertainty about meaning without making all interpretations equally
unsupported. Plant evidence, motifs, consequences, and contrasting perspectives
that players can notice and revise.

Do not require narrative closure universally. Require the ending or stopping
point to reckon with the form's promise: settlement, transformation, reflection,
aftermath, or intentionally unresolved tension.

## Design with bodies, materials, and space

Treat these as rule-bearing affordances:

- distance, visibility, audibility, occlusion, routes, thresholds, and territory;
- posture, gesture, gaze, rhythm, balance, aim, coordination, exertion, and
  fatigue;
- touch, proximity, costume, props, furniture, light, sound, weather, and site;
- who can reach, lift, read, hear, distinguish, remember, or safely perform an
  action;
- setup, reset, transport, storage, breakage, hygiene, and emergency access.

Use embodiment when it produces the experience—not merely because simulation
seems immersive. A symbolic card may create more agency and access than literal
restraint, darkness, weight, or physical pursuit.

Design spatial information deliberately. Private corners, public stages,
bottlenecks, inaccessible routes, and noisy rooms change coalition, exposure,
privacy, and participation. Walk the site from each role and access need.

Never make endurance, pain, intoxication, sleep loss, unwanted touch, or inability
to leave an implicit price of participation.

## Treat roleplay and bleed responsibly

Distinguish:

- **in-character intent** from player intent;
- **character consequence** from participant punishment;
- **productive bleed** from uncontrolled distress or relationship damage;
- **fictional hierarchy** from organizer or community power;
- **chosen vulnerability** from pressure to perform vulnerability.

Build calibration into the interaction where intensity can vary. Use clear
signals or procedures to increase, maintain, reduce, redirect, pause, or end
intensity. Select tools for the actual community, language, lighting, physical
ability, genre, and power dynamics. A tool that participants cannot remember,
perform, or safely invoke is decorative.

Make refusal easy and non-punitive. Consent to attend is not blanket consent to
every scene, topic, touch, role, or escalation. Permit withdrawal without
requiring an in-fiction justification.

For antagonistic roles, provide ways to create strong play without outsourcing
all boundary detection to the person being targeted. Brief aggressors on
responsibility, not only permission.

## Plan debrief and aftermath

Use debrief when the experience creates intense emotion, ambiguity between role
and person, unresolved social information, or a need to transition out of play.
Choose functions explicitly:

- mark that play has ended;
- separate player from role;
- surface factual misunderstandings;
- appreciate contributions without ranking emotional performance;
- identify welfare or conduct concerns and routes for follow-up;
- collect research feedback with separate consent; and
- restore space, bodies, relationships, and belongings.

Do not force public disclosure of emotion or trauma. Offer quiet, private, later,
and opt-out routes. Do not treat a cathartic debrief as proof that the design was
safe or successful.

## Diagnose common failures

- **Conversation without consequence:** players talk, but no commitment,
  information, capability, or relationship changes.
- **One charismatic engine:** one participant supplies all goals, interpretation,
  and energy while the rules leave others passive.
- **Fiction-mechanics split:** the story praises one behavior while incentives
  reward another.
- **Facilitator as life support:** the host continually invents stakes or repairs
  dead scenes.
- **Implicit social tax:** shy, disabled, marginalized, or conflict-averse
  participants must work harder for equal agency.
- **Escalation ratchet:** public attention rewards intensity with no equally
  legible way to reduce it.
- **Ceremonial safety:** a tool is announced but socially costly, inaccessible,
  or unsupported when invoked.
- **Bleed as quality metric:** distress is treated as evidence of artistic depth.
- **Prop realism over play:** material fidelity reduces access, legibility, or
  meaningful choice.

Repair the causal structure and responsibility allocation before adding more
fictional prompts.
