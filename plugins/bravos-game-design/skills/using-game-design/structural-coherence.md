# Structural Coherence

Use this reference when individual mechanics are attractive but the whole game
is flat, repetitive, fragmented, overgrown, or unable to sustain a session.

## Contents

- Five-scale ladder
- Consequence and dependency graph
- Loops and transformation
- Session arc and pacing
- Mechanic jobs and removal
- Rewiring patterns
- Replay, campaign, and closure
- Idea-garden discipline

## Inspect five scales

### Moment

Inspect the immediate act of choosing, manipulating, performing, moving,
revealing, speaking, aiming, arranging, or resolving. Ask whether it is legible,
responsive, expressive, and proportionate to its frequency.

Do not let a satisfying moment excuse a dead decision. A tactile draw, dramatic
reveal, elegant animation, or funny prompt can be excellent local craft while
remaining structurally inert.

### Decision

Inspect the trade-off at the point of action. Identify the beliefs, values,
costs, risks, and relationships that distinguish the live options. Ask whether
different reasonable players or reachable states could support different
choices.

### Loop

Inspect how outcome feeds a later decision:

```text
perceive -> model -> choose/perform -> resolve -> interpret -> changed situation
         ^                                                    |
         `----------------------------------------------------'
```

Require some transformation across repetitions: new information, capability,
position, relationship, constraint, risk, content, or player mastery. A loop
that resets completely must derive depth from execution, expression, social
variation, or discovery; otherwise repetition exposes the emptiness.

### Session

Inspect how loops accumulate into rhythm and change. Map opening, development,
escalation, reversals, breathing space, climax, settlement, and aftermath only
where those functions suit the experience. Do not force a dramatic arc onto a
meditative toy, endless sandbox, ritual, or deliberately unresolved work.

### Whole game

Inspect the promise delivered by the complete experience. Ask what becomes
possible, meaningful, understood, transformed, or complete that was not true at
the start. If replay or campaign play is intended, identify what changes across
runs besides content order.

## Draw the consequence graph

Represent nodes as decisions, performances, state changes, revelations,
relationship changes, and payoffs. Draw an edge only when one materially alters
another.

Look for:

- **dead ends:** exciting actions whose effects are never consumed;
- **sources without choices:** autonomous systems that generate spectacle but
  leave players watching;
- **sinks without origins:** outcomes that feel arbitrary because prior play did
  not prepare them;
- **single funnels:** many routes collapse into the same later state;
- **isolated subsystems:** internally elaborate rules with no cross-system edge;
- **overcoupling:** every action changes everything, making causality illegible;
- **seat gaps:** a player never changes or is changed by another consequential
  decision;
- **facilitator patches:** a human repeatedly supplies missing stakes,
  interpretation, or consequence.

Count edges only when they can change later legality, information, incentive,
capability, relationship, timing, or action ranking. A line of flavor text or a
score increment that never affects play is not automatically a causal edge.

## Identify the loop's transformation

For each repeated loop, complete:

> Repeating this loop remains interesting because **[player model, situation,
> capability, relationship, or performance demand]** changes in **[specific
> way]**, forcing **[new consideration or expression]**.

Common transformations include:

- depletion, accumulation, investment, and conversion;
- spatial expansion, enclosure, route formation, or terrain change;
- learning hidden regularities or other players' tendencies;
- building an engine, vocabulary, repertoire, or character;
- commitments and reputation constraining future action;
- escalating risk and shortening opportunity windows;
- new rule combinations and counterplay;
- physical or performative mastery;
- authored meaning accumulating through persistent traces.

If only numbers grow while decisions remain stable, progression is cosmetic.
If only content changes while the same response remains correct, the design has
a content treadmill.

## Shape session rhythm

Map pressure rather than assuming it should rise continuously. Track:

- cognitive load;
- urgency and irreversible stakes;
- social exposure and conflict;
- uncertainty and information density;
- physical exertion and sensory intensity;
- option breadth and planning horizon;
- downtime, reflection, and recovery;
- proximity to closure or transformation.

Use contrast. Constant intensity becomes a new baseline; constant low pressure
becomes drift. Let quiet phases prepare, reinterpret, or make room for the next
meaningful act rather than merely delay it.

Check transition causality. The opening should seed material that later phases
consume. Escalation should arise from decisions, accumulation, clocks, or
relationships rather than an arbitrary chapter number. Endings should reckon
with what play made important.

For open-ended forms, replace “ending” with a satisfying stopping boundary:
completed creation, resolved expedition, social ritual, exhausted possibility,
or player-chosen exit.

## Give every mechanic a job

Assign one primary job and, at most, a few compatible secondary jobs:

- create or differentiate a decision;
- expose or conceal information;
- create consequence or memory;
- regulate pace, scarcity, or escalation;
- connect players or systems;
- enable expression, performance, or fantasy;
- teach, signal, or make state legible;
- protect access, consent, recovery, or trust;
- establish setup, transition, settlement, or closure.

Apply four tests:

1. **Removal:** What player behavior and experience disappear?
2. **Substitution:** Can a simpler rule do the same job with fewer side effects?
3. **Frequency:** Is the interaction rich enough for how often it occurs?
4. **Load:** Does its cognitive, administrative, physical, social, and content
   cost fit its contribution?

A mechanic with no job is decoration. Decoration can be valuable, but label it
honestly and budget it against attention and production cost.

## Rewire before cutting

When a locally good idea lacks global consequence, try one targeted move:

- **Persist:** carry its result into later state, memory, reputation, or space.
- **Expose:** make the result legible to someone whose decision it can change.
- **Price:** connect it to a real opportunity cost or limited window.
- **Cross-couple:** let it alter another system's options without making every
  system depend on it.
- **Escalate:** make repetition transform risk, capability, or interpretation.
- **Hand off:** let one player's output become another player's input.
- **Forecast:** let it create evidence or commitments relevant to a later choice.
- **Settle:** make the ending reckon with it.
- **Compress:** merge it with a mechanic already carrying the same job.

Reject rewiring that creates more procedure than consequence. Preserve the
local pleasure in an optional flourish, scenario, expansion, or idea garden when
it cannot justify core complexity.

## Decide whether replay is part of the promise

Do not require replayability universally. For replayable games, distinguish:

- **combinatorial variation:** different setups, content, or random outcomes;
- **strategic variation:** different viable plans and opponent responses;
- **expressive variation:** different creations, roles, performances, or social
  relationships;
- **discovery variation:** new models, secrets, or interpretations;
- **campaign transformation:** persistent consequences change later play;
- **community variation:** shared knowledge, scenarios, mods, or metagames.

Variation without changed decisions is cosmetic. Infinite content cannot rescue
a solved or indifferent loop.

For one-shot or deliberately unreplayable work, test whether the single arc
earns its duration and whether irreversibility supports rather than coerces the
experience.

## Maintain an idea garden

Record shelved ideas with:

- the local pleasure or insight worth preserving;
- the global job they failed to perform;
- the cost or conflict that removed them;
- a condition under which they might become relevant; and
- the last evidence or reasoning used.

Do not keep them partially active “for later.” Dormant rules still tax teaching,
implementation, testing, and player attention.

## Diagnose global boredom

Suspect structural failure when:

- players enjoy each resolution but stop caring which option they choose;
- early and late turns ask the same question with larger numbers;
- actions create stories in debrief but no changed decisions during play;
- each subsystem is clever yet players can optimize them independently;
- escalation comes from a timer or content deck rather than accumulated play;
- the ending reports scores without resolving the tensions players experienced;
- only the active player is engaged;
- adding content improves novelty briefly but not desire to continue; or
- players understand the rules but cannot state what they are trying to make
  happen.

Use the graph and five-scale ladder to locate the break before inventing another
mechanic.
